Probability and simulation · @giuseppe1-1 ↗

Giuseppe’s probability and simulation work defines the traffic used to train and test the detector, then uses Bayes’ theorem to investigate how often an alert represents a real attack when attacks are rare.

Methods and implementation

Seeded traffic model

traffic/src/generators.js builds synthetic requests for six endpoint shapes. A categorical draw selects the endpoint: for example, search has a 25% weight and products 20%. Parameters are sampled for that endpoint, so a search has a query and page number while a cart has a product ID and quantity.

The sampling functions in traffic/src/distributions.js give the data varied, skewed shapes. Search and text words follow a finite Zipf-style ranking, so common words recur while rare ones remain possible. Page numbers and quantities use geometric distributions; comment and biography lengths use a bounded log-normal distribution, producing many shorter values and a long tail. A Box–Muller draw supplies the normal variable behind those lengths.

Reproducible splits

traffic/src/rng.js supplies the seeded random stream, and traffic/src/dataset.js uses it to generate and shuffle the samples. The model is trained on legitimate requests only; a separate legitimate calibration split selects the operating threshold. The held-out test split contains both legitimate requests and attacks, with labels and variants used for evaluation only. The same seed and code produce the same dataset.

Attack variants and legitimate hard cases

Each attack starts as a legitimate request with one parameter replaced by a payload string. Canonical examples cover known signature families; obfuscated examples transform those families, while novel families are fixed before evaluation and have no dedicated signature. Short mimicry examples challenge the detector with inputs that resemble ordinary values. These strings are measured as data.

traffic/src/hard-cases.js supplies unusual but legitimate test examples, including punctuation, non-Latin text and long input. They expose false alarms that typical inputs may miss. The dashboard sets the hard-case share to 5% of legitimate test requests; this is an experimental choice, not a measured share of real traffic.

From simulation to Bayes

dashboard/src/base-rate-data.js combines the test split’s measured detection and false-positive rates with a hypothetical attack share. Bayes’ theorem then estimates the positive predictive value: the fraction of alerts expected to correspond to attacks. This makes the cost of false alarms visible even when the detector catches a high proportion of attacks.

The traffic distributions and attack mix are assumed models, not measurements of a particular website. Results depend on the selected seed, sample size and mixture, so they illustrate the experiment rather than establish real-world performance.

The curve uses the measured true-positive and false-positive rates from this held-out test split, then varies hypothetical prevalence. It is a scenario, not a new experiment.

Reading the curve

The horizontal axis is the hypothetical percentage of requests that are attacks; its logarithmic scale makes rare attack shares visible. The vertical axis is positive predictive value (PPV): the percentage of alerts expected to be actual attacks. Hover over the curve to inspect a point. The test split provides the true-positive rate (TPR, attacks caught) and false-positive rate (FPR, legitimate requests flagged); the curve holds both rates fixed as prevalence changes.

How the PPV formula works

The formula estimates the probability that an alert is an actual attack. Its numerator represents attacks caught; its denominator includes both attacks caught and legitimate requests incorrectly flagged. The graph applies this calculation at each hypothetical prevalence, keeping the measured rates fixed.

PPV = (TPR · prevalence) / (TPR · prevalence + FPR · (1 − prevalence))

  • PPV (positive predictive value): the probability that an alert corresponds to an attack, or the expected share of alerts that are true positives.
  • TPR (true-positive rate or sensitivity): the share of attacks detected. Together with prevalence, it determines the fraction of all requests that are true-positive alerts.
  • FPR (false-positive rate): the share of legitimate requests flagged. False alarms increase the denominator and reduce PPV when attacks are caught.
  • Prevalence: the share of all requests that are attacks. At lower prevalence, legitimate requests make up more of the population, so false alarms can account for a larger share of alerts.

Results for this run

What the result means

When both measured rates are positive, the curve rises with prevalence: if attacks are rare, false alarms among the much larger legitimate population can outnumber the attacks caught. A higher PPV means a larger share of alerts are useful, but it does not tell us how many attacks were missed; recall and the false-negative rate matter too.

The curve assumes the measured rates stay the same at every hypothetical attack share. They come from one synthetic test split, and a different mix or seed can change them. In particular, observing zero false positives in a finite sample does not prove that the true false-positive rate is zero.
